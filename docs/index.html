How often the forecasts were right

The river model learned from 1997–2020 and was then run day by day through the 2021–2026 monsoons. Each year was forecast with only the years before it. The score is the critical success index (CSI), the standard grade for flood warnings: hits divided by hits, misses and false alarms together. 1 is perfect.

Mapping floodwater through monsoon cloud

Clouds hide floods from ordinary satellites. Radar does not: calm floodwater reflects the signal away and shows up dark. A U-Net trained on 252 hand-labelled Sentinel-1 scenes outlines the water. These four are from Assam and were never used in training.

How it works

Four steps, each tested on data it never trained on.

  1. Read the rivers

    Daily flow at six Brahmaputra stations from Dibrugarh to Dhubri, plus the Subansiri, Jia Bharali, Dhansiri, Kopili, Beki and Barak (GloFAS, 1997–2026).

  2. Learn how surges travel

    Water passing Dibrugarh reaches Guwahati days later. A gradient-boosted model uses every station's recent rise to forecast flooding 1–7 days ahead.

  3. Predict impact, not just water level

    A second model turns river forecasts and each district's flood history into the chance that people will be affected, trained on ASDMA's district reports.

  4. See the water from space

    A U-Net maps flooded land from Sentinel-1 radar, so forecasts can be checked against where water actually spread.

Data: river discharge from GloFAS v4 via Open-Meteo · flood reports from ASDMA's daily FRIMS bulletins, compiled by CivicDataLab's IDS-DRR project · radar scenes and labels from Sen1Floods11 (Bonafilia et al., 2020) · district boundaries from the 2011 Census shapefiles.
